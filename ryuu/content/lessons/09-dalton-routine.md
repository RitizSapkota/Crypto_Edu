### Before the session

1. **Where is value?** Mark the prior value area, POC, high and low.
2. **How has value been moving?** Higher, overlapping, unchanged, lower. That is the context from Dalton 4.
3. **What did the last session leave behind?** Single prints, poor highs or lows, a late spike, a POC far from the close.
4. **Is there a larger balance?** If several sessions overlap, mark the edges of the whole area. Those edges matter more than any one day's levels.
5. **Was inventory lopsided?** A P or b shaped session suggests covering or liquidation that may be finished.

### During the session: two questions

Dalton reduces monitoring to two questions, asked again and again.

| Question | How to read it |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Which way is the market trying to go?** | Range extension beyond the initial balance, which end has the tail, where periods are closing, and the rotation factor. |
| **2. Is it doing a good job of it?** | Is volume expanding in that direction, and is value following price? Good: value builds at the new prices. Poor: price goes, value stays behind. |

A market that is trying to go up and doing it well is one to join. One that is trying to go up and doing it poorly is one to watch for failure. That second case is the exhaustion and absorption read from the tape lesson, seen through structure.

### The rotation factor

A simple count for the first question. For each 30-minute period, compare it with the one before:

- High above the previous high: plus one. High below: minus one.
- Low above the previous low: plus one. Low below: minus one.

Add them up across the session. A clearly positive total means the rotations are working upward, a negative one downward, and a total near zero means balance.

### After the session

- What day type was it, and when could you have known?
- Where did value end up against yesterday's?
- Did the close sit at an extreme or in the middle?
- What is unfinished and likely to be revisited?

### Adapting it to bitcoin

| Dalton, futures | Bitcoin |
| --- | --- |
| One pit session a day | Three sessions a day: Asia, London, New York. Profile each one. |
| The open | Each session open, plus the daily and weekly open. |
| Initial balance: first hour of the day | First hour of each session. Mark IBH and IBL per session. |
| TPO only | TPO beside volume and delta. Volume data is clean in crypto, so use it. |
| Overnight gap | The weekend. Thin trade, weak structure, tends to be given back. |
| Commitment of traders, floor colour | Open interest, funding and liquidations. |

> **Limits.**
>
> The framework was built on pit-traded futures in the 1980s. No one has published a test of it on bitcoin that we could find, and the tests on S&P futures in Dalton 3 are not flattering. Use it for what it is good at: a shared language for structure and a disciplined order of questions.

### Check yourself

<details>
<summary>London breaks above its first-hour range and makes new highs for three periods, but the volume profile keeps its fattest node back at the open. Which of the two questions is failing?</summary>

The second. The market is trying to go up, but value is not following price. That is poor performance, and a failure back into the opening range is the thing to watch for.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why profile each session on bitcoin and not just the UTC day?</summary>

Because liquidity arrives in three waves with different participants. A day profile blends them and hides structure, such as single prints, that is clear inside one session. It is also why session prints tend to be more reliable than daily ones.

</details>
