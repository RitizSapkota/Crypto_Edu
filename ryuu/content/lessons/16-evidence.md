### The base rate

| Study | Sample | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chague, De-Losso, Giovannetti (2020) | Brazil index futures, 2013 to 2015. 1,551 people who day traded for 300+ days. | 97% lost money. 1.1% earned more than the minimum wage. |
| Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean (2014) | Every day trader on the Taiwan exchange, 1992 to 2006. | Fewer than 1% were predictably profitable after fees. |

This does not mean it cannot be done. It means the default outcome is a loss, and anything you believe about your edge needs evidence from your own trades.

### What has support

- **Order flow moves price, and thin books move more.** Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014) found that short-horizon price changes track order flow imbalance closely, and that the impact is larger when the book is shallower. That backs the core logic here: empty book after a cascade, absorption at stacked bids, walls slowing price.
- **Intraday momentum exists in some markets.** Gao, Han, Li and Zhou (2018) found that the first half hour's return predicts the last half hour's in US equity index funds. That fits the idea that a trend out of a session open can persist.
- **Bitcoin shows both intraday momentum and reversal.** Wen, Bouri, Xu and Zhao studied hourly bitcoin returns from 2013 to 2020. Momentum showed up mainly during US equity hours and reversal mainly outside them, and the effects were stronger in illiquid hours and weaker on days with jumps. The results are before trading costs.
- **Order book signals are real but very short-lived.** A 2026 study of Binance perpetuals found order flow imbalance and spread predicted the next 3 seconds of price. In its backtest the edge was not significant for BTC, and latency was not modelled. That is a machine's horizon. For a manual trader it supports reading flow as context, not as a signal you can race.
- **Forced flow overshoots.** The mechanism behind liquidation fades is sound. We found no study that puts a win rate on it.

### What is unverified

- The 80% rule. One reported test: 5 wins from 8 strict setups in about 1,000 sessions. Far too few to mean anything.
- Opening types and day types as signals. A 455-session study in S&P futures found no clear edge.
- Kill zone claims, including "80% of weekly trends are set on Tuesday in London". No source is given anywhere.
- The first test of a level being close to a promise.
- Single print fill rates, and session prints beating daily ones.
- The range retest as free money.
- Accuracy claims in commercial indicator guides. They rarely give a sample, a win rate or risk rules.
- That scalping has the highest expected value. Often asserted, never shown with numbers.

### Costs decide at low timeframes

Say your venue charges 0.05% per side as a taker. A round trip costs 0.10% before slippage. On a scalp that targets 0.30%, a third of the gross is gone. At a 50% win rate with equal-sized wins and losses, that trade loses money. Work this out for your own fees and typical target before judging any setup.

### Contradictions in this material

1. **Confirm first, or be early.** Price action methods want confirmation. Knife catching bids before it. Pick one per trade type.
2. **Fat nodes: stall or slip.** Covered in the gap lesson. Unresolved.
3. **Single prints: target or support.** Depends on whether the move is still live.
4. **Time is noise, and session opens matter.** Both are used. Be clear which job each does.
5. **Indicators last, and an indicator-heavy toolkit.** Fine, if indicators only ever support a price read.
6. **Mechanical and highly accurate, and you need to feel it out.** The fade is described both ways in the same paragraph.
7. **Buy the bounce, and bottoms take time.** Bottom hunting in selloffs is sold as the best expected value trade. Volume analysis says V bottoms are rare and most people cannot time one. Both hold only if the bounce trade is a quick scalp.

### How to find out

1. Pick one setup. Write its trigger, stop and target in one sentence each.
2. Tag every trade of it in the journal, including the ones you took badly.
3. Do not judge it early. After 30 trades, an observed 60% win rate could really be anywhere from about 42% to 78%. After 100 trades the range narrows to about 50% to 70%.
4. Judge it on average R after costs, not on win rate alone.

None of this is financial advice. It is a study guide built from your sources and public research.
